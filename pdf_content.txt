ABSTRACT  
This  final  year  project  presents  DALILI,  a  web-based  AI-powered  symptom  checker  
for
 
early
 
risk
 
assessment
 
of
 
malaria
 
and
 
pneumonia
 
in
 
Kenya.
 
The
 
system
 
uses
 
machine
 
learning
 
models
 
(decision
 
trees
 
and
 
random
 
forests)
 
trained
 
on
 
local
 
datasets
 
from
 
KEMRI,
 
MoH
 
DHIS2,
 
and
 
KNBS
 
to
 
differentiate
 
symptoms,
 
provide
 
risk
 
levels
 
(Low/Moderate/High/Emergency),
 
location-specific
 
advice,
 
educational
 
content,
 
and
 
nearest
 
facility
 
guidance
 
in
 
English
 
and
 
Kiswahili.
 
The  problem  of  delayed  diagnosis  in  rural  areas  was  addressed  through  Agile  
development,
 
with
 
core
 
features
 
including
 
a
 
symptom
 
questionnaire,
 
Django
 
and
 
Flask
 
and
 
FastAPI
 
backend
 
API
 
for
 
ML
 
inference,
 
React
 
frontend,
 
and
 
PostgreSQL
 
database.
 
Ethical
 
considerations
 
include
 
clear
 
non-diagnostic
 
disclaimers
 
and
 
compliance
 
with
 
Kenya
 
Data
 
Protection
 
Act
 
2019.
 
Evaluation  involved  pilot  testing  with  over  60  users  (university  students  and  
community
 
members),
 
achieving
 
model
 
accuracy
 
≥85%
 
on
 
test
 
data,
 
and
 
high
 
System
 
Usability
 
Scale
 
(SUS)
 
score,
 
and
 
positive
 
feedback
 
on
 
trust
 
and
 
usability.
 
The  project  demonstrates  practical  AI  application  in  Kenyan  public  health,  counters  
global
 
model
 
biases,
 
and
 
aligns
 
with
 
the
 
Kenya
 
Digital
 
Health
 
Strategy
 
2020–2025
 
and
 
UHC
 
goals.
 
Recommendations
 
include
 
USSD
 
expansion,
 
mobile
 
PWA
 
for
 
offline
 
use,
 
and
 
integration
 
with
 
MoH
 
systems.
 
Keywords:  Malaria,  pneumonia,  symptom  checker,  machine  learning,  Kenya,  early  
risk
 
assessment,
 
digital
 
health.

CHAPTER  1:  INTRODUCTION     Background  of  the  Study    
Malaria  and  pneumonia  are  among  the  most  significant  public  health  challenges  in  
Kenya,
 
contributing
 
substantially
 
to
 
morbidity
 
and
 
mortality,
 
particularly
 
in
 
vulnerable
 
populations.
 
According
 
to
 
the
 
Kenya
 
National
 
Bureau
 
of
 
Statistics
 
(KNBS,
 
2024),
 
malaria
 
accounted
 
for
 
9,541
 
deaths,
 
while
 
pneumonia
 
led
 
to
 
23,710
 
deaths,
 
representing
 
over
 
11%
 
of
 
all
 
registered
 
deaths
 
nationwide.
 
These
 
figures
 
highlight
 
the
 
disproportionate
 
impact
 
on
 
children
 
under
 
five,
 
who
 
make
 
up
 
a
 
significant
 
portion
 
of
 
the
 
affected
 
demographic,
 
with
 
pneumonia
 
being
 
the
 
leading
 
cause
 
of
 
death
 
in
 
this
 
age
 
group
 
globally
 
and
 
in
 
sub-Saharan
 
Africa
 
(WHO,
 
2023).
 
In
 
Kenya,
 
malaria
 
alone
 
is
 
responsible
 
for
 
approximately
 
70%
 
of
 
outpatient
 
visits
 
in
 
endemic
 
areas,
 
and
 
pneumonia
 
complicates
 
up
 
to
 
20%
 
of
 
severe
 
malaria
 
cases,
 
creating
 
a
 
deadly
 
overlap
 
in
 
symptoms
 
and
 
outcomes
 
(KEMRI-Wellcome
 
Trust,
 
2022).
 
The  burden  is  exacerbated  by  socioeconomic  factors,  including  poverty,  limited  
healthcare
 
infrastructure,
 
and
 
geographical
 
barriers
 
in
 
rural
 
and
 
peri-urban
 
areas.
 
Rural
 
populations,
 
which
 
constitute
 
about
 
70%
 
of
 
Kenya's
 
inhabitants,
 
often

delays
 
in
 
accessing
 
diagnostic

due
 
to
 
long
 
distances
 
to
 
health
 
facilities—sometimes
 
exceeding
 
10-20
 
km—and
 
high
 
transportation
 
costs.
 
This

to
 
increased
 
mortality
 
rates,
 
as

is

Economically,

an
 
estimated
 
1.3%
 
of

and

(Ministry

Pneumonia

KES
 
10

Historical  trends  show  a  decline  in  malaria  prevalence  from  8%  in  2015  to  6%  in  2020

(Kenya

Pneumonia,

(KNBS,

The

Community

Existing  challenges  include  low  health  literacy,  where  only  40%  of  Kenyans  recognize

diseases
 
(MoH
 
Kenya,

on

self-medication,  which  often  leads  to  antimicrobial  resistance.  Global  tools  like  rapid
